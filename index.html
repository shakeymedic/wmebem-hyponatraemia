Hyponatraemia in the ED

1. Introduction & Clinical Imperative

1.1 The Scale of the Problem

Hyponatraemia (serum sodium <135 mmol/L) is the most common electrolyte disorder in clinical practice, affecting 15-30% of hospitalised patients. Its prevalence increases with age, making it a frequent finding in the ED.

It is an independent predictor of increased morbidity, mortality, and length of hospital stay across various conditions like heart failure, cirrhosis, and pneumonia. Even mild hyponatraemia (130-134 mmol/L) is associated with impaired cognition, falls, and poorer outcomes. Think of it not as a lab anomaly, but as a marker of significant physiological stress.

1.2 Pathophysiological Principles

Fundamentally, hyponatraemia is a disorder of water balance, not sodium deficiency. It's a state of relative excess of total body water compared to total body sodium content. This is almost always due to impaired water excretion, driven by the persistent secretion of Arginine Vasopressin (AVP), also known as Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH).

Normally, low plasma osmolality suppresses AVP, allowing the kidneys to excrete large volumes of dilute urine. In hyponatraemia, this mechanism fails. The primary danger is neurological. Low extracellular tonicity drives water into brain cells, causing cerebral oedema. Encased in the rigid skull, the brain cannot swell, leading to raised intracranial pressure and symptoms ranging from headache to seizures, coma, and brainstem herniation.

Key Principle: The rate of fall in sodium is more critical than the absolute number. A rapid drop is a neurological emergency, whereas a chronic, slow decline may be well-tolerated due to cerebral adaptation.

2. Classification & Initial Assessment

2.1 Classification of Hyponatraemia

By Severity (UK Guidelines)

  • Mild: 130-135 mmol/L
  • Moderate: 125-129 mmol/L
  • Severe/Profound: <125 mmol/L

By Chronicity

  • Acute: Developed over <48 hours. Risk = Cerebral Oedema.
  • Chronic: Present for >48 hours (or unknown duration). Risk = Osmotic Demyelination with rapid correction.

Assume CHRONIC if duration is unknown. This is the safest approach.

By Plasma Tonicity

  • Hypotonic (<275 mOsm/kg): True hyponatraemia. Most common type.
  • Isotonic (275-295 mOsm/kg): Pseudohyponatraemia (e.g., hyperlipidaemia).
  • Hypertonic (>295 mOsm/kg): Redistributive (e.g., hyperglycaemia).

2.2 The Critical First Look: Symptoms Dictate Management

Treat the patient, not the number! The immediate priority is identifying life-threatening neurological compromise.

Severe Symptoms (Neurological Emergency)

  • Seizures
  • Reduced GCS (≤8)
  • Persistent vomiting
  • Cardiorespiratory arrest

Action: IMMEDIATE treatment with hypertonic saline.

Moderately Severe Symptoms

  • Confusion
  • Headache
  • Nausea (without vomiting)

Action: URGENT treatment with hypertonic saline.

Mild or Absent Symptoms

Lethargy, anorexia, poor balance. These patients require systematic investigation, not emergency sodium correction.

2.3 Structured Clinical Assessment

History

  • Symptom Onset: Acute vs. chronic.
  • Fluid Balance: Vomiting, diarrhoea, fluid intake (polydipsia?), recent surgery.
  • Medication Review: CRITICAL. Look for diuretics (especially thiazides), SSRIs, antiepileptics (carbamazepine), PPIs, NSAIDs.
  • PMH: Heart failure, liver cirrhosis, CKD, malignancy (especially lung), CNS disorders, Addison's, hypothyroidism.
  • Social/Dietary: "Tea and toast" diet, beer potomania, recreational drugs (MDMA).

Examination: Volume Status Assessment

Clinical assessment of volume status is notoriously unreliable. Use it to form a hypothesis, but confirm with labs (urine sodium).

  • Hypovolaemia: Tachycardia, postural hypotension, dry mucous membranes, low JVP.
  • Hypervolaemia: Raised JVP, peripheral oedema, ascites, pulmonary oedema (bibasal crackles).
  • Euvolaemia: Absence of the above signs. A diagnosis of exclusion.

3. A Stepwise Diagnostic Pathway

Time-Critical Action: Obtain paired blood and urine samples BEFORE administering any significant volume of IV fluids. Post-treatment samples are uninterpretable.

3.1 Essential Initial Investigations

Blood Tests

  • U&Es (for Na+, K+, renal function)
  • Serum Osmolality
  • Blood Glucose
  • LFTs
  • TFTs
  • 9 am Cortisol (if adrenal insufficiency suspected)

Urine Tests (Paired with Bloods)

  • Urine Osmolality
  • Urine Sodium Concentration

Correcting Sodium for Hyperglycaemia

In hyperglycaemia, measured sodium is falsely low. Use this calculator to estimate the corrected sodium.

3.2 Diagnostic Algorithm

Step 1: Assess Serum Osmolality - Classify as hypotonic, isotonic, or hypertonic.

Step 2: Assess Urine Osmolality (in hypotonic hyponatraemia)

  • <100 mOsm/kg: AVP is suppressed. Kidneys are working. Cause is excess free water intake (primary polydipsia, low solute intake).
  • >100 mOsm/kg: AVP is active. This is pathological. Proceed to Step 3.

Step 3: Integrate Urine Sodium & Clinical Volume Status

Use a threshold of Urine Na+ 30 mmol/L. This step differentiates the causes of AVP-mediated hyponatraemia.

3.3 Differential Diagnosis by Volume Status

Volume Status Urine Sodium Key Causes
Hypovolaemic <30 mmol/L Extra-Renal Loss: Diarrhoea, vomiting, burns, pancreatitis.
>30 mmol/L Renal Loss: Diuretics (thiazides), Addison's, cerebral salt wasting.
Euvolaemic >30 mmol/L SIAD, glucocorticoid deficiency, severe hypothyroidism.
Hypervolaemic <30 mmol/L Congestive heart failure, liver cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome.
>30 mmol/L Acute or chronic kidney disease.

Focus: Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuresis (SIAD)

SIAD is the most common cause of euvolaemic hyponatraemia. It is a diagnosis of exclusion with the following criteria:

  • Hypotonic hyponatraemia (Serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg).
  • Inappropriately concentrated urine (Urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg).
  • Clinical euvolaemia.
  • Elevated urine sodium (>30 mmol/L) on normal salt/water intake.
  • Normal renal, adrenal, and thyroid function.

Common causes of SIAD include: Malignancy (SCLC), pulmonary disease (pneumonia), CNS disorders (stroke, bleed), and numerous drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine).

4. Emergency Management of Symptomatic Hyponatraemia

This is for patients with severe or moderately severe neurological symptoms ONLY. The goal is to rapidly increase serum sodium by a small amount to reverse cerebral oedema, not to normalise the level.

4.1 The 2022 Society for Endocrinology Protocol

The recommended method is intermittent bolus therapy with 3% hypertonic saline, not a continuous infusion. Boluses are faster, more effective, and paradoxically safer, with a lower risk of over-correction.

1. Initial Bolus: Administer 150 mL of 3% hypertonic sodium chloride IV over 20 minutes.

2. Target: Aim for a 5 mmol/L rise in serum sodium in the first hour.

3. Repeat if Needed: If severe symptoms persist, repeat the 150mL bolus up to two more times. Check serum sodium within 60 minutes of the first bolus.

Stopping Criteria for Hypertonic Saline

Stop bolus therapy as soon as ANY of the following are met:

  • Neurological symptoms resolve.
  • Target rise of 5-6 mmol/L is achieved.
  • Total rise approaches the 24-hour limit of 10 mmol/L.
  • Serum sodium reaches 130 mmol/L.

5. Management Strategies Based on Aetiology

5.1 Hypovolaemic Hyponatraemia

Goal: Restore intravascular volume to switch off the AVP stimulus.

  • Treatment: Fluid resuscitation with IV isotonic crystalloids (e.g., 0.9% sodium chloride).
  • Rate: Based on severity. A 500mL-1L challenge over 2-4 hours is reasonable for significant dehydration.

High Risk of Autocorrection: Once volume is restored, AVP switches off, leading to a massive water diuresis (aquaresis) and a dangerously rapid rise in sodium. Monitor serum sodium frequently (every 2-4 hours) after starting fluids. A sudden increase in urine output is a red flag.

5.2 Hypervolaemic Hyponatraemia

Goal: Achieve a negative water balance and treat the underlying organ failure.

  • First-Line: Fluid restriction (<1000 mL/day) and dietary salt restriction.
  • Second-Line: Loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide) to promote free water excretion.
  • Definitive: Optimise management of the underlying heart failure, cirrhosis, or renal disease.

5.3 Euvolaemic Hyponatraemia (Primarily SIAD)

Goal: Counteract the inappropriate AVP action.

  • First-Line: Fluid restriction (<1000 mL/day, sometimes as low as 500-750 mL).
  • Essential: Search for and treat the underlying cause (e.g., stop offending drug, treat infection).

Critical Negative: DO NOT give 0.9% saline to a patient with SIAD. They cannot excrete the free water and will retain it, while excreting the sodium. This will paradoxically WORSEN the hyponatraemia.

6. Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (ODS)

6.1 Pathophysiology

ODS is a devastating, iatrogenic complication of the rapid correction of CHRONIC hyponatraemia. In chronic states, brain cells adapt by extruding organic solutes (osmolytes) to prevent swelling. If external sodium is then raised too quickly, the extracellular fluid becomes hypertonic relative to the osmolyte-depleted brain cells. This pulls water out of the cells, causing them to shrink, triggering inflammation and destruction of myelin sheaths, classically in the pons (Central Pontine Myelinolysis).

The risk of ODS in ACUTE hyponatraemia is virtually non-existent, as cerebral adaptation has not occurred.

6.2 Identifying the High-Risk Patient

Certain patients are exceptionally vulnerable. Adopt a more conservative approach from the outset in these groups.

Risk FactorRationale
Initial Serum Na+ ≤120 mmol/LMore profound osmolyte depletion and larger required correction.
Alcohol Excess / Alcohol Use DisorderDepletion of the organic osmolytes needed for adaptation.
MalnutritionDepletion of substrates (amino acids, vitamins) required for the adaptive response.
Severe Liver Disease (Cirrhosis)Disruption of normal brain osmoregulation due to hyperammonaemia.
HypokalaemiaPotassium is an effective osmole; its deficiency impairs cell volume regulation.

6.3 Prevention: The Safe Correction Corridor

UK & European Safety Limits

Do NOT exceed 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours.

Do NOT exceed 8 mmol/L in any subsequent 24 hours.

High-Risk Patients:

For patients with risk factors for ODS, proactively aim for a more conservative target of <8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours.

6.4 Managing Over-Correction

Exceeding safe correction limits is a medical emergency requiring immediate, active intervention.

  • Recognise Early: A sudden increase in urine output (>100-200 mL/hour) often heralds rapid over-correction.
  • Stop the Correcting Agent: Immediately discontinue any 0.9% or hypertonic saline.
  • Contact a Specialist: Urgent discussion with Endocrinology or ITU is mandatory.
  • Actively Re-lower Serum Sodium: Administer hypotonic fluid (IV 5% Dextrose or oral/NG free water).
  • Consider Desmopressin (DDAVP): This synthetic AVP acts as a pharmacological "brake" on the kidneys, halting free water excretion and allowing for a controlled re-lowering of the sodium.

7. Disposition & Specialty Liaison

7.1 Admission Criteria

Admit the majority of patients with significant hyponatraemia. Specific criteria include:

  • Any symptoms attributable to hyponatraemia.
  • Biochemically severe hyponatraemia (Na+ <125 mmol/L).
  • Hypovolaemia requiring IV fluid resuscitation.
  • Acute onset hyponatraemia.
  • A new underlying diagnosis requiring admission.
  • Any diagnostic uncertainty.

7.2 Critical Care (ICU/HDU) Referral

Immediate referral to critical care is required for:

  • Severe neurological symptoms (Seizures, GCS ≤8).
  • Any patient requiring treatment with IV hypertonic saline.
  • Haemodynamic instability.
  • Patients who have over-corrected and require active management.
  • Patients at extremely high risk of ODS requiring very frequent monitoring.
